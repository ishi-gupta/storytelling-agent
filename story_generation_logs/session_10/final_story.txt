
==================== SCENE 1 ====================
Predawn made the flats a sheet of pewter. Their headlamps cut cones through rills of salt, catching the slick green curls they’d come for.

“Skær,” Alma said, crouching. “Perfect edges.”

Signe’s waders sucked free of the mud with a sigh. “Tide’s lifting. Ten minutes and this skerry’s a rumor.”

“Eight,” Alma said, teasing a tuft loose, rinsing it in the black mirror of water. “One more patch.”

“We’ve said ‘one more’ three times.” Signe tapped her watch. The beam of her lamp trembled on a flecked boulder. “You feel that push?”

Alma lifted a frill to her mouth, bit. Saline cracked, sweet as cucumber peel, then a clean iron hum. She smiled around it. “It’s singing.”

“It’s saying leave.” Signe pointed east where the horizon was whitening. “There.”

They waded—hips brushing eelgrass, boots nudging hermit crabs—toward a hummock tufted like a monk’s crown. Signe held the crate steady as Alma clipped and rinsed, clipped and rinsed, building a neat green fan. Water crawled higher, lapping their knees.

“Done,” Signe said, breath tight.

“Done.” Alma snugged the lid, the crate whispering with brine. On the phone, she dropped a pin—Skerry E/Dragør, spring 04:38—then flicked off her lamp. The world softened to gulls and the crate’s gentle slosh as they slogged back, grinning.


==================== SCENE 2 ====================
First light pooled in the lee of the glacial stones, a quiet pocket out of the wind. They set the crate there. The sea hissed like someone shushing a room.

“Cold fingers?” Signe asked.

“Warm mouth,” Alma said, lifting a frill. She bit. Salt burst, mineral and sweet. Heat moved up her throat and, suddenly, she was in the Dragør kitchen: enamel chipped at the stove lip, dill pollen dusting a breadboard, the wet-wool reek of nets steaming off a peg. Her mother’s knuckles tapping a jar. “Smag nu.”

Signe watched her face change. “What did it do?”

“It put me back,” Alma said, laughing once, breathless. “Right there. The yellow window. Rye heel with butter. It’s—me.”

“That’s what I heard,” Signe said softly. “In you.”

Alma opened her phone. The dropped pin glowed. “Kaspar will want this.”

“Kaspar wants everything.” Signe’s smile didn’t reach her eyes. “Do you?”

Alma looked at the slick green fan, then at Signe. She edited the note to a tide and a time, nothing more. “Not yet.”

Signe nodded. “Not yet.”

Alma wrapped a small tuft in waxed paper and slipped it into her pocket. The windbreak held, the light climbed.


==================== SCENE 3 ====================
Late morning light slid across the empty dining room. An office table had been dragged to the center; clipboards waited like placemats.

Kaspar didn’t sit. “New NDAs. Effective immediately. No sharing foraging coordinates, internally or externally. Coordinates are company IP.”

The FOH lead began passing pens. Paper rasped.

Signe didn’t take one. “You’re claiming rocks and tides?”

“I’m protecting product,” Kaspar said. “You’re paid to harvest, not publish maps.”

“That coast isn’t your pantry.”

“It is when it’s on my menu.” He tapped the packet. “Sign, or I pull your day-rate and work with someone who understands alignment.”

Alma signed, small and neat. Mateo hovered, then scrawled his name.

Kaspar turned to him. “Capacity planning: assume growth past our permit ceiling. Prep for it. Model the numbers.”

“That’s… beyond our allowance,” Mateo said.

“Your models don’t go to jail. Make them.”

Einar, jaw set, signed. The FOH lead stacked the pages methodically.

Signe’s pen stayed capped. “I share seasons with the community. Tide windows, beds. That’s stewardship.”

Kaspar’s voice cooled. “Not here. Here, you share when and where I say.”

Silence pooled. Signe uncapped the pen, signed hard enough to tear the carbon, and slid it back without looking up. Her mouth was a line. Everyone exhaled.


==================== SCENE 4 ====================
Noon heat pressed the back kitchen flat. The dish pit hissed; a sprayer needled steel. Kaspar capped a marker with his thumb and pointed at the whiteboard.

“Quietly,” he said. “Past the permit. Seats, courses, prep loads. I need capacity plans that don’t exist on paper.”

Mateo rubbed a damp palm on his apron. “We’re already kissing the amperage. The combis pull thirteen kilowatts on a retherm, the mixers spike. The breaker panel’s old. Wastewater—ours salinity, oils—hits the trap hard. The limit is the limit.”

“Workarounds,” Kaspar said. “Staggered runs, night cycles, rent a silent genny if we must. Greywater capture, pre-filter bags. Make the numbers fit.”

“That shifts heat into the night and moves our load, it doesn’t erase it,” Mateo said, uncapping a blue pen. He drew boxes: ovens, induction, washer. “If we add one more phase draw, the panel will chatter. And extra brine means more TDS down the line. The city samples at random.”

Kaspar leaned in. “Then we never look like we’re peaking. Smooth the spikes. Hypotheticals, not SOP. You and me—verbal.”

Mateo stared at the grid. The dish sprayer clicked off. “Fine. I’ll model scenarios and keep them verbal.”

“Good,” Kaspar said. “Erase as you go.”


==================== SCENE 5 ====================
Afternoon heat hummed through the prep kitchen. Trays of shaved kohlrabi glowed like coins. Alma tapped a ramekin. “Skær there, three threads. Dill pollen like breath, not sneeze. Kohlrabi fans on the diagonal. Brine last.”

Einar slid closer with a towel over his shoulder. “You want the shallow bowls or the flat slate?”

“Shallow,” Alma said. “I want the brine to catch light.”

Mateo spooned brine over a test slice, tasted, winced. “Too sharp. It knifes the kohlrabi.”

“It’s yesterday’s ratio,” Alma said.

“Today’s skær’s hotter,” he said, already reaching for a quart of dilution. “Fifteen percent down, two drops honey, cold.”

Line cooks watched, hands parked over tweezers. A compressor throbbed. Someone hummed along with the radio.

Alma tasted. “It still snaps.”

Mateo swirled, added a splash from the rinse bucket, stopped, shook his head, grabbed a clean pitcher, measured from the filtered tap instead. “My bad. Clean water. Reset.”

He dipped, tasted, nodded. “Now it rounds.”

Alma set the sequence: “Kohlrabi fan, skær threads, drizzle, pollen, then cap with brine—twenty seconds to service.”

Einar swapped bowls to a chilled rack. “Run the line?”

“Run the line,” Alma said.

The team moved as one.


==================== SCENE 6 ====================
The room dipped into a hush as the first bowls hit the pass, brine gleaming.

FOH slid in, headset crooked. “We’re pacing too tight. Two tables just sat. Give me three minutes.”

“Window’s twenty,” Alma said, eyes on the line. “Send.”

“Alma—”

“Send.”

Einar nodded; servers lifted, six bowls gliding into the dark wood dining room.

At table eight, a woman touched her spoon to the kohlrabi, inhaled, then covered her mouth. Tears slipped anyway. Across the aisle, a man blinked hard and laughed once, helpless. Chairs creaked; napkins rustled. The hum of conversation folded smaller around the shallow bowls.

Leif leaned over his plate, nose to the brine, then tasted. He stilled, eyes glassy, and sat back.

“Sir?” the captain whispered.

Leif wiped his cheek, embarrassed, grinning. “Do you have a second reservation card? This week. Any night.” He tapped the rim. “I need to come back.”

FOH, arriving with backup menus, paused. “We can try—”

“Make it happen,” Alma said from the pass, not looking up. “Next flight.”

Servers swept again. Table twelve reached for hands across candles. A low chorus rose—“What is this?”—and then the answer rippled: “The skær.” The room leaned in.


==================== SCENE 7 ====================
Cold rolls from the walk-in, fogging Kaspar’s glasses as he blocks the threshold with a tray of cambros. “We need more skær. Double the pulls by Friday. And I want full coordinates access.”

Signe stands half in the chill, oilcloth notebook clamped under her arm. “No.”

Alma hovers in the doorway to the office, steam curling from her mug. “Kaspar, capacity isn’t the same as the tide. We can scale prep. We can’t scale the moon.”

Kaspar taps the tray. “Investors don’t eat the moon. They eat plates. Give me the charts. I’ll have Mateo model windows.”

Signe’s jaw flexes. “Mateo can model the weather too? The rocks don’t care about your model. I cut what the beds can spare.”

“Coordinates, Signe.” His smile is managerial and tight. “We’re one team.”

“We’re not a trawler,” she says. “You already made me sign your papers. That’s enough.”

Alma lowers her voice. “Kaspar, post limits; Signe sends windows, not raw pins. We protect the beds and still make service.”

“Half data is no data,” he says.

Signe backs out of the cold. “Then you have no data.”

Kaspar exhales. Alma looks at the notebook. No one moves.


==================== SCENE 8 ====================
The alley hums with the chug of a pallet jack and the metallic clack of the delivery bay door. Heat bounces off the bricks; gulls bicker over a torn bread bag.

Kaspar steps in front of Signe, one hand on the rusted rail. “We scale or we starve. Volumes, coordinates, full share.”

Signe wipes salt grit from her fingers onto her oilcloth notebook. “You scale prep. I scale nothing. The beds breathe. I take what they can spare.”

“You’re blocking growth,” he says. “Rights to supply sit with the company under your NDA.”

“My rights sit with me. The coast isn’t an asset column.”

He lowers his voice. “You don’t get to throttle output because you’ve got feelings about rocks.”

“They’re ecosystems. And I’m not throttling; I’m stewarding.”

He laughs once, sharp. “This is insubordination.”

“It’s boundaries.”

“Give me the pins or I’ll source elsewhere.”

“From who? The beds are finite. The law is, too.”

A forklift beeps past. Sweat slips down her temple. She tucks the notebook under her arm, steps around him.

“Signe,” he calls.

She keeps walking, out to the street’s glare. Behind her, the bay door shudders closed. Inside, orders stack with nothing to feed them.


==================== SCENE 9 ====================
Second seating hums like a low tide. Glassware chimes. FOH glides a path for Leif, who sits again at the corner two-top facing the pass.

Server: “Back so soon?”
Leif: “To watch.”

Alma plates at the pass, quick wrists, quiet eyes. A runner drops the kohlrabi disks, skær blurred like sea breath, dill pollen dusted in a faint comet.

Leif doesn’t touch it yet. He scans the room: a couple clasping knuckles; a solo diner staring at steam as if it might speak; a four-top gone suddenly quiet. He lifts a slice, chews, closes his eyes. His jaw stills.

He rises, steps to the pass between fires.

Leif: “Where?”
Alma: “North and near.”
Leif: “Which beds?”
Alma: “The ones that consented.” A dry smile. Her tongs click. “We don’t take more than the memory can carry.”

FOH shifts closer, gentle but present. Tickets shuffle. A diner stifles a laugh, then a breath breaks into a small cry.

Leif: “Harvest window? Salinity?”
Alma: “Enough to be itself.”

He nods, returns to his seat, finishes with measured bites. The room glows—the soft hush after a good chord. He sets down his fork, stands, leaves a card under the glass, and walks out without a word, eyes bright, cheeks wet.


==================== SCENE 10 ====================
Neon from Torvehallerne’s stalls washes Leif’s ceiling in fish-scale flicker. He types like he’s keeping time.

Phone on speaker. Editor: “You’re filing at midnight?”

Leif: “I ate the tide. Running now.”

Editor: “It reads like a benediction. We can’t anoint a pop-up at this volume. Cool it, publish tomorrow with a hedged—”

Leif: “No hedge. Run it before the morning markets open. My byline, my risk.”

Editor: “Overhype kills trust.”

Leif: “Then let my trust stand trial. Three hundred words. Clean. No metaphor soup.”

A buzz. Kaspar’s text: “Embargo? Capacity is… fluid.”

Leif: “No embargo,” he texts back. “Keep your seats honest.”

Editor: “If we flood them and it collapses—”

Leif: “It won’t. Hit publish.”

Silence. The apartment hums: cooler fan, a scooter below, someone laughing in the alley.

Editor, softer: “You’re staking the masthead.”

Leif: “I’m staking my name.”

A click. The CMS chime. Editor: “It’s live at dawn, homepage slot.”

Leif exhales, adds the kicker: “Skær, tasted like a door opening.”

Kaspar again: “Site already pinging. Are you—”

Leif: “Dawn,” he replies, and kills the screen.

Grey seeps into the windows. At 05:02, his phone stutters—pings stacking, then a continuous vibration. A booking widget squeals. A server pings: “We’re full by noon.” Another: “Waitlist at 300.” Leif smiles toward the waking market and finally closes the laptop.


==================== SCENE 11 ====================
Fluorescents flickered over concrete and pipes. The Contractor thumped the blue tank into place, breath fogging in the cold.

Kaspar: “Quiet. If anyone asks, it’s a greywater buffer.”

Einar checked the door, then the stairwell. “No one’s in. It’s only gulls up there.”

Contractor, tightening a union: “Feed line’s tapped at the seawall. Your harbor’s basically a vein.”

Kaspar: “Keep it clean, keep it moving.” He stepped around a mop sink, palm on the tank’s acrylic dome. “Recirc on a loop, right?”

Contractor: “Loop and return. You wanted the bypass straight to drain—”

Kaspar: “For purge only.”

Footsteps. Mateo slid in, hair wet from the mist. “You ran a cross-connection? Where’s the backflow preventer?”

Kaspar: “Negative pressure only if we idle. We won’t idle.”

Mateo crouched, tracing PVC with a finger. “No check on the return, no absolute on the intake. That mesh won’t stop bloom or diesel sheen. If the harbor burps, it’s in our tank.”

Einar: “He can add a valve.”

Kaspar: “He can add it later.” To the Contractor: “Prime it.”

Mateo: “Kas—this is off HACCP, off permits.”

Kaspar: “It’s off the books. Throw the switch.”

The pump whirred. Water rushed, the dome pearled with microbubbles. Einar muted an alarm with his thumb. Paperwork lay as blank as the clipboard beneath it.


==================== SCENE 12 ====================
Pallet jacks clicked in the loading bay, distant beeps echoing off concrete. The tank hummed like a throat clearing.

Kaspar: “No label, no clipboard. If it needs attention, you tell me. Mouth to ear.”

Einar traced a finger along a union, eyes on the film skimming the dome. “Filters foul. Pumps seize. We need a log. Temperatures, salinity, backflush dates.”

Kaspar: “You want a diary for an unlicensed vein tied to the harbor? You want it subpoenaed? No. You watch. You remember.”

Einar: “HACCP—”

Kaspar: “Is for the dining room. This is a convenience.”

A forklift rattled past the bay door, cold air pushing in. Einar’s breath plumed, then flattened. “We at least put checks in prep? A daily list.”

Kaspar: “Verbal. Mateo can complain into his coffee. You keep it running.”

Einar wiped his palm on his apron. “Then I need the key.”

Kaspar dangled a small brass key from a red tag. “Only you.”

They moved into the staff corridor—scuffed linoleum, an exit map curling on the wall. Einar knelt at a cleaning caddy, lifted the false bottom beneath bleach and rags, and slid the key inside.

Einar: “Verbal only. I’ll remember.”

Kaspar: “Good. Now forget.”


==================== SCENE 13 ====================
Steam ghosted over the prep bench. Mateo had a bread pan full of brine, a printed circuit taped into a deli lid, and two silicone tubes wriggling to peristaltic pumps.

Alma set a cambro on the dish station. “How close?”

“Close-ish.” Mateo dipped the probe. Numbers jittered, then slid. “It drifts two points every minute. Temp comp’s off.”

“I need 2.8, flat. Kohlrabi goes limp over three.”

He pulled a thermometer, pinched its lead to the board. “Okay, we marry them.” He swapped a resistor, thumbed a trim pot. The LED steadied, then wandered again.

Alma: “Service in four hours.”

Mateo rolled his sleeve and snapped open a bottle labeled 2.00 mS/cm. “Two-point. Trust me.” He plunged the probe, tapped a button. “Zeroed.” He moved to a 5.00 standard. “Slope.”

The pumps clicked like ticking glass. “Controller, target two-point-eight.” The salt line flexed; a drip of harbor-bright brine fell in, then stopped.

Alma watched the digits: 2.76… 2.81… 2.79.

She slid a coin of kohlrabi in, fanned it, tasted. “Snap’s back.”

Mateo grinned. “Baseline stored. Tolerance point-one.”

Alma wiped the rim. “Lock it. Next bin.”


==================== SCENE 14 ====================
The tide board glowed over the pass: 18:12–18:19 SKÆR, 18:26–:33 PICKLES, 18:41–:48 BROTH.

Einar snapped a timer. “Green two-top, kohlrabi in six. Skær fires on my seven.” He pointed without looking. “Freja, you’re late.”

The FOH lead leaned in, headset crooked. “Hold fourteen; they’re mid-story.”

Tickets cleared. The board flipped to the next window, and the kitchen moved with it.


==================== SCENE 15 ====================
Pipes thrummed like a chest with a bad cough. The new tank burped, then shrieked a flat alarm that bit the concrete.

“Kill intake,” Mateo said, already shoving through damp air to the panel. “Purge on three. One—”

“Don’t,” Einar snapped, boots sloshing a shallow sheen across the floor. “We’ll lose the lot. Ride it.”

The tank coughed again, a dry, ragged gulp. Alma flinched. “It’s pulling air.”

“Squall pushed the surge,” Mateo said. “Harbor’s frothing. We close or we seed the brine with whatever’s in that brown foam.”

Einar slapped the gauge. “Hold product. The window upstairs is tight.”

“The window downstairs is safety,” Mateo said, hand on the red paddle. “Alma?”

Alma stared at the sight glass: bubbles churning like spit. “How long to purge?”

“Three minutes. Then we reset salinity.”

“Three minutes is a course,” Einar said. “Kaspar said hold.”

The room ticked around them—alarm, drip, the shallow hammer of water. Alma wiped her palms on her apron. “Pause intake. Now.”

Mateo pulled. The alarm pitched down. The pipes slowed to a wet heartbeat.

Einar swore, already dialing. “Kaspar? We paused. Pressure’s dropping.”

“Good,” Mateo said, watching the gauge ease. “Keep it there.”

Alma exhaled, quick. “Stay on the line. Tell him we need clearance to purge.”


==================== SCENE 16 ====================

“Put me on speaker,” Kaspar said, voice thin through Einar’s wet phone. “What’s the number?”

“Zero intake, forty-two salinity, pressure falling,” Mateo said. “We purge, then reset.”

“We don’t purge,” Kaspar snapped. “We’re not dumping a service. Restart. Full cycle, then bleed the air.”

Mateo shook his head. “Harbor’s soup. Backflow will seed the brine. Protocol says wait.”

“Protocol won’t seat sixty covers,” Kaspar said. “Restart.”

“No,” Mateo said. “I’m responsible for this line.”

“You’re responsible to this house,” Kaspar said, ice in it. “Einar, do it.”

Einar grimaced at the sight glass. “It’s foaming like a latte.”

“Do it,” Kaspar repeated. “Mateo, log a variance. I’ll sign.”

Mateo planted himself at the panel. “I’m not authorizing.”

“You’re not needed,” Kaspar said. “Einar.”

A beat. Then Einar slid past, thumbed the breaker, and yanked the intake paddle. The pump shuddered to life, a harsh throat-clear. Alarms flickered, steadied. The gauge climbed. The foam thinned to a clean spin.

“See?” Kaspar said. “Stable. Salinity?”

“Forty-two point two,” Einar read. “Pressure holding.”

Mateo watched the return line tremble, a fine buzz in the pipe. “Stable on paper,” he said, jaw tight. “Write your variance.”


==================== SCENE 17 ====================
Rain hammered the tin roof. The lights hiccupped—once, twice—and the pump coughed to a stop. In the dim, the tank room went dead quiet except for the squall’s roar.

“Power blip,” Mateo said, hand on the panel. “Hold. Let the surge pass.”

Kaspar’s voice came sharp from Einar’s phone on a crate. “Restart. We lose the skær, we lose service.”

Alma burst in, soaked, wind at her back from the dock door. “Wait. We don’t know what the backflush routes in a blackout.”

“It routes to the scrubber,” Kaspar said. “Mateo, cycle.”

“Not with harbor soup in the intake,” Mateo snapped. “We need clarity.”

“Clarity is sixty covers,” Kaspar said. “Restart.”

On the back dock, line cooks in aprons held the door against the rain. “We’re dying on tickets,” one called. “Chef?”

Alma’s eyes went to the braided hoses, the Y-valve labeled STORM DRAIN. “Kaspar—this system auto-dumps on reset.”

“Cycle,” Kaspar repeated.

Einar swallowed and threw the breaker. The pump surged. Valves clicked. Somewhere below, a heavy gasp. A sour, briny burp shot through the waste line and out beneath the dock, lost to the rain.

Mateo watched the meter spin. “How much just backflushed?”

“Keep it stable,” Kaspar said.

On the dock, the drain grate rattled and ran brown.


==================== SCENE 18 ====================
A bream rolled belly-up against the ladder. Another drifted into the lane ropes. The harbor bath lifeguard fired a whistle and pulled red tape across the entrance.

“Out, out—baths are closed,” she said, voice flat from repetition.

A pair of joggers slowed, staring. “That smell,” one muttered. “Like batteries and brine.”

Einar crouched at the canal edge, phone low, framing the gray-brown plume uncurling from the outfall by the restaurant pilings. “Hold still,” he told the water, as if it could. He panned to the dead fish, then back to the pipe.

“Hey!” The lifeguard stepped over, palm out. “Behind the tape.”

“I’m documenting the source,” Einar said.

“You’re documenting a public health closure,” she shot back. “Back.”

He edged a foot past the cordon. She gently but firmly shoved his shoulder. “I said behind.”

Joggers in neon cut a wider arc, pinching noses. “City’ll say it’s algae,” one said. The other pointed at the plume. “Algae doesn’t come out of a pipe.”

Einar kept filming. “I need the intake in frame. And the outfall.”

“You need to respect the barrier,” the lifeguard said, taping the last post. “Baths are shut until further notice.”

On his screen, a thread of murk fingered out from the restaurant’s drain, straight into the lane of white fish. He held the shot. Captured it. Ending.

Lockers thunked like muted drums. In dry storage, sacks rustled and dill pollen leaked its green-amber scent. Freja cupped a pipette over her tongue, eyes closed, breath held. A paper shot glass of water chased it.
