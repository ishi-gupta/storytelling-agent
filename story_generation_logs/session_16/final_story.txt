
==================== SCENE 1 ====================
The hot hive row simmered, lids shining like coins. Maeve slid the veil back to her neck and stood still, letting the bees draw lazy figure-eights around her wrists.

“Easy, ladies,” she murmured. “I’m just rubbernecking.”

A flicker under the pallets snagged her eye—soft brown, a tremor of breath. The cottontail eased itself forward, one hind leg dragging a rhythm in the dust. It tucked under a crossbeam where the shade held tight.

Maeve crouched. “Hey there.”

A guard bee butted her veil. She held steady, watching the rabbit’s ears lift—listening—and then flatten again. Between the pallet slats: a nest of crushed clover, a marble of droppings, a curled leaf dragged in like a blanket. Not a fluke, then. A path showed itself if you looked: scuffed powder from the burdock, a thread of fur hooked on blackberry, the twist it took around the water tote to get here.

“You picked the hottest street in town,” she said. “Smart. Skunks don’t love traffic.”

Field rules ran through her like an audit: No feeding, no baiting, no attractants near the hives. The laminated sheet on the warm room door. Eli’s handwriting, blocky: Close gates. No snacks outside.

“Not a snack,” she told the empty air. “A kindness.”

A forager landed on her sleeve, fanning. Heat bled up from the boxes; honey smell, waxy-sweet, carried a hum in it like a held breath. The rabbit’s nose twitched. Its bad leg shivered and tucked.

Maeve stood, brushing her knees. “You don’t need me hovering.”

She backed down the row, counting lids, counting breaths, already shaping something small and green in her mind. Romaine. No crinkle bags. Later. Quiet.


==================== SCENE 2 ====================
The yard had gone to charcoal and whisper. Maeve clicked her headlamp to red and cupped it, a dim coin in her palm.

“In and out,” she told herself. “Easy drop. Nobody’s snack. Nobody’s business.”

Romaine leaves rode warm against her ribs, tucked under her hoodie. She slid between hive stands. The boxes hummed like a sleeping engine; a few bees bumped her veil—pap, pap—then settled.

“Hey, ladies. Night shift.”

She knelt by the hot row and eased a leaf under the pallet slats. A tremor in the dust. The cottontail’s ears rose, turned, flattened. She slid another leaf, fingers slow, knuckles grazing wood.

“Green room service,” she whispered. “No tip necessary.”

The rabbit ghosted forward. In the red spill, she could see the twitch of the nose, the hitch of the bad leg. She held her breath, then her hand, palm-down, hovered just above its side. Warmth through the fur. A soft piston breath, steady, steady, steady.

“That’s right,” she murmured. “Take your time.”

A car far off hissed down the highway. The fence charger ticked—tick, tick—from the corner post. She tucked a third leaf under the crossbeam and backed away on her heels, slow as sap.

“Done,” she said to the hum. “No witness. No harm.”

Warm room next. She slipped inside, letting the door catch without a click. Heat wrapped her, honey-thick; the dehumidifier gave a low, steady drone. Laminated rules sheet watched her from the nail.

“Yeah, yeah,” she told it.

She fished out the produce sleeve and the crimped twist tie. The plastic whispered—too loud. She winced, stuffed both deep into the box of bent foundation offcuts, slid a cappings towel over top, and squared the lid.

“Clean,” she said, palms flat on the metal. “No trail.”

Outside, the night held. Back at the hives, a leaf rasped softly under wood.


==================== SCENE 3 ====================
Morning laid a clean line along the pasture. Eli thumbed the come‑along ratchet. “Click and hold,” he told the corner wire. “No sag, no invitations.” The brace creaked, the strands sang a higher note. He tugged each insulator, slapped a palm on the post, stepped back.

From a bucket he fished foil squares, smeared each with bacon grease till they shone. “Loud and ugly,” he said, folding one over the top wire at the corners. “Touch this, rethink your life.” He spaced them like flags, wiped his fingers on a rag, then clipped the tester to the hot and ground.

The charger ticked. The screen blinked 8.7. “Hot,” he said, satisfied. He dragged the earth rod once with his boot, leaned, checked again. “Hot is hot.”

At the hive row he set the tub, the scoop, the alcohol, the timer. “By the book,” he told the bees. He knocked a frame twice, bees avalanching into the cup. “Half cup, girls, no heroes.” He poured them into the jar, screwed the lid, filled to the line with clear sting of alcohol.

“One minute.” He swirled. “Thirty seconds.” He swirled. He rapped the lid over the white pan and counted the russet commas as they slid. “Two. Copy.” He rinsed, set the timer again. “Next.”

He moved down the line, voice steady with the steps. “Measure, shake, swirl, count.” He dabbed numbers on a Rite‑in‑the‑Rain page: yard, hive ID, mites per hundred, date. He flipped open the spiral, added alcohol, foil, grease, and a new gate handle to the expense side with quick block letters. “Paper trail,” he said. “No drift.”

At the perimeter he squinted once more at the foil gleam, tapped the top wire with the tester, watched the green bars climb. “Stay out,” he told the fence. “Stay in,” he told the bees. He capped the pen. “Counts logged.”


==================== SCENE 4 ====================

Eli slid the spiral onto the kitchen table’s scarred corner and nudged aside a salt shaker and a jar of rubber bands. “Alright,” he told the calculator, thumbing it awake. “No drama. Just math.”

Receipts fanned out like leaves—feed store, fuel, hardware, co‑op printouts misted with propolis smudge. He flattened each with the heel of his palm. “Foil, grease, gate handle,” he read, ticking the boxes he’d added in the yard. “Alcohol, too. Don’t forget the alcohol.”

He wrote the date crisp at the top: Day 2. In the margin he kept a running skeleton of the month: honey invoices, jar purchase, the co‑op’s net terms, the last diesel fill. “Fuel’s drifting,” he said. “Next trip will bite.” He circled FUEL in a thin, stubborn oval.

He stacked the feed slips together. “Protein patty, pollen sub. July’s always hungry,” he told the empty chair across from him. “Feed’s a plug, not a plan.” He starred FEED, then drew a small arrow toward the week’s end.

The calculator clicked under his thumb. He murmured the totals as if checking a gate latch. “Two eighty‑four, ninety‑nine, sixty‑two. Tax. Round up.” He cross‑checked the bank app, the ledger columns, the spiral’s expense side. “Deposit pending,” he noted, then penciled a narrow buffer line and underlined it twice.

He leaned back, rubbed his eyes. “Margin’s a belt notch tighter,” he said quietly. He added a bracket: FUEL + FEED—watch.

He totaled once more, capped the pen, and squared the spiral on the table’s grain. “Books balanced,” he said to the little office nook and its humming fridge. “We’re fine if we act like it.” He snapped a paperclip on the receipts, slid them home, and let the room settle around the numbers.

==================== SCENE 6 ====================
Headlights washed the barn silver, engine ticking. Two women in fleece leaned out the sedan with grocery bags, plastic clatter and a sweet wet smell of spring mix. “Hi! Is this the bunny place?” one called, grinning.

Eli stepped into the beams, palms up. “No. It’s a working yard. You can’t be here.” His voice carried over the idle’s thrum.

Aiden came out of the shadow of the hay bay, smile already up, phone in hand. “Hey, hey, it’s fine. Quick look from the drive. No flash, no touching—”

“Aiden,” Eli snapped, not looking at him. “You invited them.”

The driver lifted a family-size box of baby carrots. “We brought treats. My kid’s asleep, but—”

“No feeding,” Eli said. “Pack it up.” He softened it half a notch. “Please.”

Aiden slid in front of him, palms splayed like he could smooth air. “It’s community, man. People love a story. It’s good for us.”

“For us?” Eli’s laugh had an edge. “You put the farm on a map for strangers in the dark. Get out of the driveway.”

The women faltered, glancing between the brothers. “We can… come back?” the passenger tried.

“You can not,” Eli said. He took a step, and something in his face made them flinch. Doors thumped; the sedan eased into a three-point turn, crunching gravel. A bag tipped in the shuffle and a scatter of carrot tops fell, green and damp on the stones.

Maeve, small and still by the bay door, crouched as the taillights swung. She scooped the greens quick, slid them into her hoodie pocket without a rustle, eyes lowered.

Aiden huffed. “You didn’t have to go full bouncer.”

“You didn’t have to sell our place for clicks,” Eli said, quiet and hard. “Take it down. All of it.”

Aiden’s mouth tilted. “It’s already out.”

“Then fix what you can.” Eli turned toward the dark lane. “And stop talking.”


==================== SCENE 7 ====================
Mist clung to the pasture edge, the south fence beaded with dew. Warden Gary came up the lane with a clipboard and a handheld tester swinging from his wrist.

“Morning,” he said, not wasting tone. “We’re done with snacks and visitors. This is an official wildlife-feeding warning.” He slid a yellow sheet into a sleeve and zip-tied it to the corner T‑post. “Posted.”

Eli nodded once. “We told them to stop. It’s our yard.”

“Doesn’t matter who started it,” Gary said. He clicked the tester to the top wire. The wand chirped and flashed. “Seven-three here. Walk it.”

They moved along the south span. Grass licked the lower wire. Henry came behind with a bucket and three rigs: two #2s and a snare looped neat on a stick.

“Under my conditions,” Gary called back without looking. “Offset jaws, pan tension at three pounds, and flag the sets. No exposed bait. No catch circles near the fence.”

“I know the dance,” Henry said. He knelt by a trail cut under the woven wire, sifted dry soil through his fingers. “Two footholds on the travel, one snare at the gap.”

“Put a tag on each,” Gary said. “And text me a pin.”

At the far span the tester flickered, then fell to two-one. Gary scowled, checked the ground clamp, kicked a mat of creeping charlie off the bottom wire. “You got load here. Charger’s bleeding.”

“I’ll clear it,” Eli said. His jaw worked. “We had a hit last night.”

“And you’ll get a citation if I see so much as a lettuce leaf,” Gary said flat. He watched Henry bed a trap, firm. “Pan check.”

Henry pressed; the jaw didn’t twitch. “Three pounds.”

“Good.” Gary straightened, wiped dew off the warning sleeve with his palm. “You’re on the line now. Stay on it.”

Eli stared at the weak blink of the tester and the bright new sheet on the post. “We don’t have room to miss.”


==================== SCENE 8 ====================
Late afternoon leaned warm on the hives. Eli set the smoker down, thumbed the bellows till sweet smoke curled, and slipped his hive tool under the super. “Easy, ladies,” he said through his veil. He popped the propolis seal, nudged the box sideways, got his knees under it, and heaved.

The weight bit deep. Half a step, his left shoulder stabbed hot, and he hissed. “No, no—” The box tilted; bees rose in a ragged roar. He caught the corner against his thigh and muscled it to the cart, breath chopped, face damp. He stood bent, hand clamped at his collarbone.

“Stupid,” he told the grass. “You know better.” He shook out his arms, tested a slow circle. Pain flared sharp and slick. “Fine. Short pulls. Singles. You win.” He set the next super on its edge, slid it instead of lifting, lips peeled back, moving like a man under a low ceiling.

By dusk the cart held a stingy stack. He closed the lids, smoked the air clear, and limped for the house.

At the kitchen table he peeled off the veil, iced his shoulder with a bag of peas, and stared at the spiral. He tore a strip of athletic tape, braced the roll with his teeth, wrapped his shoulder and upper arm till the tape squeaked. “Temporary fix,” he muttered. “Temporary.”

He opened the notebook. “Pull 4B,” he said aloud, pen ticking. “H-3: minus one super, heavy. H-7: queenright but light. H-12: chalkbrood, two frames. H-14: drone-laying—call it lost.” He paused, ink shining. “Deadout: Nuc 2.” He drew a hard box around the word.

He tapped the total, rubbed his forehead, and added another line. “Feed: two pails. Expense.” The numbers sat there, dull and honest. He closed the book with his palm and stared at the taped shoulder like it belonged to someone else.


==================== SCENE 9 ====================

“Hold the beam steady,” Lila said, snapping on nitrile gloves at the mudroom workbench. The barn cat lay draped like a rag, purring through gritted teeth, a fist-sized burr of fur shaved to a bald button.

Maeve swallowed. “I hate that it breathes.”

“That’s why we remove it,” Lila said. “Saline. Flush.”

Maeve irrigated the puckered hole. A pearl of whitish gristle flexed. The headlamp made everything too close, too bright.

“Forceps,” Lila said.

Maeve passed them and braced the cat’s chest. Lila teased the rim, slow, precise. The larva bulged, ringed with tiny hooks.

“Ugh—stop—no, don’t stop,” Maeve muttered.

“Deep breath. It’s not a horror, it’s a passenger,” Lila said. “There. Twist.”

The grub slid free like a fat seed. Lila dropped it in a jar. “Betadine.”

Maeve flushed again, hands starting to feel useful. Lila packed gauze, wrapped, taped. The cat licked once, offended, then settled.

“Clean wound,” Lila said, stripping her gloves. “No more passengers.”

Maeve exhaled. “Health one, infestation zero.”

“Temporary if you don’t change the conditions,” Lila said. “Let’s step outside.”

They moved by headlamp along the fence corridor, breath fogging. Lila clipped a stake flag at a corner. “We build cover here, not by the hives. Brush pile every twenty yards. Think like water—flow, not puddle.”

“Redirect the drift,” Maeve said, planting another stake. “Leave gaps for runways.”

“And block the shortcuts to the pallets,” Lila said. “Dog-rose, blackberry, dead limbs stacked teepee style. Low roofs, tight walls.”

Maeve sketched on a damp notepad, marking Xs for piles, arrows for corridors. “Control on this side, drift on that.”

“Exactly.” Lila pressed a final flag into the dark soil. “Map made. We make it real in daylight.”


==================== SCENE 10 ====================

Pre‑dawn dim seeped under the feed shed door. Maeve lifted the latch with her sleeve and clicked her headlamp on. Shelves came up in hard circles of light—pallets of sugar, sacks of pollen sub, the little crate tucked behind the lick tubs. Her secret.

“Okay,” she said to the quiet room. “We’re done with this.”

She hauled the crate onto the bench. Romaine hearts, carrots, parsley, all rubber‑banded, beaded with cold. She slit the bands, dumped greens into a contractor bag, the wet thud loud as a scold. “Convenient doesn’t mean smart,” she told the pile. Knot, twist, knot again. Out to the bin, lid slammed, latch clipped.

Back inside, she rolled two steel drums forward, wiped their gaskets, seated gamma lids. “In, not out,” she said, funneling dry sugar in a clean hiss. Pollen patties slid from torn cardboard into airtight tubs. She stripped every box with a smile on it, flattened, taped for recycling. The headlamp lit dust motes like gnats; she sneezed, then kept going.

In the honey room, the air was sweet with old work. Cappings had scattered under the bottling table, a freckle of wax and crumbs. Maeve pulled the shop vac down, checked the filter, and traced the nozzle along baseboards, into corners, under the sump. “No more buffet.” She scraped the extractor lip, bagged the scrapings. Floor got a bleach mop, then hot water, then dry squeegee.

She stacked sealed tubs on a pallet, slid the drums against the wall, labeled them with a marker: SUGAR—SEALED; POLLEN—SEALED. The room looked sparse, edged, quiet.

Maeve clicked off the lamp and listened to the new stillness. “Short term loss,” she said, locking the door. “Lowered draw.” She pocketed the key and moved to the next latch.


==================== SCENE 11 ====================
Morning snapped clean over the garden edge. Aiden lifted the controller; the drone rose with a waspish whine.

“You’re gonna spook him,” Maeve said, kneeling by the parsley bed. Soot ranged in a tight zig under the hedgerow, tail flagging.

“I’m at sixty percent throttle,” Aiden said. “Acoustic footprint’s fine.”

Soot froze, head low, then cut left. Brush ticked. Aiden tipped the drone forward. “Contact. Hedge gap by the elder.”

Maeve slid a cinder block from the compost corner. “Hold him wide, not deep.”

“He’s already deep.” Aiden angled for a shot. The rabbit exploded from a matted tunnel, gray and quick, stitching the fence line. Soot yipped once and checked himself on Maeve’s sharp “Leave!” He danced sideways, nose burning the air.

“Track his swing,” she said.

“South, along the burdock, then—wait—under the crushed wire. He’s favoring the left, see it?” The drone’s shadow skimmed, wobbled over berries and nettle.

Maeve plucked three sprigs, tucked them under the cinder block’s lip so scent bled but didn’t shine. “Come back path, not chase path,” she muttered.

“He’s in the thorn pocket,” Aiden said. “Pausing. Grooming.”

“Then that’s his pivot.” She nudged the block tight to the rabbit’s likely pass, half-buried in leaf duff. Soot snuffed the seam and sneezed.

“Mark it?” Aiden asked.

Maeve set a snapped twig crosswise on the fence staple. “Visual for us, not for him.”

The rabbit burst again, took the same seam by the elder, vanished under the crushed wire. Aiden exhaled. “Route confirmed.”

“Good. Kill the buzz.”

The drone settled, silence spilling back. Soot stood quivering, ears like sails. Maeve dusted her hands. “Low and quiet now. Let him own that lane.”

Aiden grinned. “Control without push.”

“Evasion without panic,” she said, standing. “We’re set.”


==================== SCENE 12 ====================
Night took the charge and gave it back. The fencer ticked steady, a pulse in the weeds. Then a thunk, low and blunt—corner post shivering. Wire sang, snapped grass, a wet huff. The young bear shouldered once, twice; staples sighed free. Plastic lids skittered, one flipping to the thistle patch. Cold poured into open boxes; the brood tone shifted thin and frantic, then faded to a chill whisper. The bear nosed the bacon foil, snorted at the sting, and rolled off into the dark, fence belly-dragging behind.

At first light Eli stepped over the drag, shoulder a hot rope. “Easy, girls,” he said, voice flat. He put his palm to a hive wall, felt the weak tremor of the cluster. Two lids cocked, one gone. He found it face-down, wet with dew, propped it on his thigh and eased it home. “Hold.” Bees spilled and resettled like poured sand.

He traced claw scuffs in the soft earth, a smear of fur on a loosened staple. “Corner’s down,” he muttered. “Two compromised.” He cinched a strap across the worst one, slid a shim under the cracked rim, set a brick to keep the wind from lifting it again. The fencer still clicked, wire dead on the ground.

“List,” he said, thumb in his pocket spiral he didn’t bother to pull. “New post. Bag of staples. Tensioner. Two lids. Charger check. Feed warmers.” He lifted another cover, winced as his shoulder caught. Bees rose, then settled to his breath.

On the pallet edge a clean green scent lifted, bright and out of place. He bent, sniffed the boards. Parsley. He straightened, eyes on the corner gap, and slid the brick a hair tighter. Repairs, then calls. No more talk. Work.


==================== SCENE 13 ====================
Headlamps swept the boxes like slow moons. The yard hummed thinly; the fencer ticked in the weeds, stubborn as a metronome. Eli’s breath smoked in the beam. Maeve stood with her hands shoved in her jacket, watching bees seep and settle around the gaps.

“I put the parsley there,” she said.

He didn’t answer at first. He set the flashlight on an inverted lid, glow pointing down so it made a little pool between their boots. “How long.”

“Since the romaine. I dumped the stash after Lila’s talk, but—last night. Under the block.”

His jaw moved once. Then he picked up the light, clicked it lower, and set it back down. “Okay.”

“Okay?” Her voice shook.

“I’m mad,” he said. “But we’ve got boxes open and a fence dragging. Anger’s not gonna tighten a staple.”

She nodded, swallowed. “I’m done feeding.”

He pulled his phone, thumbed Lila’s name, hit speaker. The ring buzzed against the lid. Night wind combed the grass.

“Tell me you’re not calling from the hives,” Lila said, sleepy and dry.

“We are,” Eli said. “We need a plan.”

Maeve leaned in. “I fed it. I’m done. We need cover away from the pallets.”

“I’ll bring loppers and flagging at first light,” Lila said. “Fifty feet from the fence minimum.”

Maeve exhaled. “I’ll sweep crumbs, clear cappings, stash all scent.”

“And no more feeding,” Eli said, looking at her.

“No more,” she said.

The three of them listened to the fence tick. Then Eli killed the speaker. “Brush tomorrow.”

“Brush tomorrow,” Maeve echoed.


==================== SCENE 14 ====================
Wind slapped the boxes sometime past two. A crack like a snapped broomstick, then the fence went quiet. Maeve sat up in the dark, listening to bees lift and settle, a low, skittering roar, and then nothing.

By gray light, they were in boots, breath showing. The corner post leaned like a folded knuckle. Two lids skewed open, inner covers clawed sideways. Frames gaped. Bees draped themselves in a slow, confused sheet.

“Damn it,” Eli said, hand on the folded post. “It bent right at the staple.”

Maeve nudged a lid with her wrist, looking at the brood rimmed in pearl gone dull. “They’re cold.”

“Yeah.” He set the lids straight, thumped them with the heel of his hand. “Charger’s still hot. It just—” He cut himself off, eyes moving along the muddy scuff outside the wire. “We don’t have the joules for that.”

Her phone pinged. Aiden’s reel: a glossy slow-pan of the tilting post and bees boiling, captioned: “Wild night at the honey ranch. Wildlife wins again.” Comment hearts bloomed.

“You have got to be kidding me,” Eli said. “He was here?”

“He said he was ‘nearby.’” Maeve swallowed. “I’ll call him.”

Eli’s phone buzzed first. Gary: Formal written warning attached. Continued wildlife attractants and fence failures will result in citation. Post suggests negligence. Expect follow-up.

Eli stared at the PDF header. “Negligence?”

Maeve’s buzzed next. Mara: Saw the reel. Customers don’t like ‘chaos honey.’ If this goes on, I’m shifting your shelf to the bottom or off for a week. Clean it up.

“Chaos honey?” Eli barked a laugh that wasn’t a laugh.

Maeve looked at the brood again, the gray on the edges. “We lost this box.”

“Two,” he said, jaw tight. He lifted the stapler. “We’re fixing the post now.”

“And the comments?” she asked.

“Let them talk,” he said, already kneeling. “We have to keep the rest alive.”


==================== SCENE 15 ====================
Frost filmed the lids at dawn. Maeve set a bucket under the lean-to and started stripping everything sweet. Foil flags came off, bacon grease into a trash bag, cappings scraped and double-bagged. Her breath made small ghosts.

“Charger reads hot,” Eli said, meter chirping. “We re‑string, brace the corners, then pull. No snacks, no scents.”

“I got it,” Maeve said, wiping the pallets with vinegar water. “Nothing left to argue with.”

Lila swung through the gate with a bundle of cuttings. “Brush wall, far side,” she said. “Stack tight. Rabbit lanes low, bear blind high.”

They worked without wasting words. Eli pounded a fresh brace, ran a new lead to ground, then snapped the switch. The fence ticked, crisp as a metronome.

“That’s music,” Lila said. “Maeve, toss me more maple. Denser.”

By noon, the cover stood knee‑thick, twig ends knit like ribs. They pulled supers quick and clean, everything that smelled wrong left on. The stack in the cart was modest, frames lacquered and light.

“Smaller,” Eli said.

“Clean,” Maeve answered.

In the shed, the room warmed and hummed. Eli uncapped; Maeve steadied the gate; honey ribboned true, no flecks, no foam. Lila boxed jars, labels square.

Maeve’s phone rang. “Mara,” she said, putting it on speaker.

A calm voice: “I saw the morning. Looks orderly. We’ll hold your shelf—steady allotment, same as last month.”

Eli looked up. “Thank you.”

“Keep it tight,” Mara said, and clicked off.

They loaded the last case as dusk thinned the frost to shine. A tremor in the brush made them turn. The rabbit slipped under the new weave, ears tucked, vanishing into the tangle.

Lila smiled. “Home.”

Eli checked the fence once more. “Hot,” he said.

Maeve capped a jar and set it on the bench. “Right.”
