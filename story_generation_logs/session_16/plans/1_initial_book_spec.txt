Genre: Contemporary realist wildlife drama
Place: Small honey farm on the outskirts of Brattleboro, Vermont, USA
Time: Late summer into early fall, present day
Theme: Human–wildlife coexistence; consequences of indulgence; boundaries and care; rural economics vs. compassion; ecology of small farms
Tone: Grounded, observational, quietly tense with moments of tenderness
Point of View: Close third alternating between Maeve and Eli; occasional tightly limited sensory passages from the rabbit without anthropomorphic thoughts 
Characters: Maeve Kelleher — 34, co-owner, marketing/processing, secretly feeding an injured cottontail, craving to nurture; Eli Barrows — 37, third‑generation beekeeper, pragmatic, cash‑strapped, prioritizes hive survival and fences; The Rabbit — adult eastern cottontail with a torn hind leg; Ruth Barrows — Eli’s widowed mother, old‑school frugality, urges hard choices; Lila Martinez — local wildlife rehabilitator, firm on non‑feeding boundaries; Gary Dutton — state game warden, cites and warns about wildlife feeding; Henry Pike — neighboring vegetable grower, sets box traps after crop losses; Aiden Cho — 16, part‑time farmhand, social‑media posts escalate attention and foot traffic; Soot — aging border collie mix, unpredictably flushes wildlife; Mara Levin — food co‑op buyer, presses for steady supply and fall promo commitments
Premise: As Maeve quietly nurses a limping wild rabbit that has begun sheltering beneath the hives on the small honey farm she runs with Eli outside Brattleboro, her compassion collides with Eli’s need to protect their bees and fall honey pull; the feeding she starts brings predators and a bear to the fence line, damages gear, and risks their year’s income, forcing the pair to decide what care looks like—habitat and hard boundaries, relocation, or a kill—as first frost nears and the farm’s ecology and finances tighten.
