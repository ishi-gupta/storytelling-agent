Genre: Contemporary realist wildlife drama 
Place: Small honey farm on the outskirts of Brattleboro, Vermont, USA - Five-acre mixed field and hedgerow parcel off a dirt spur from Hinesburg Road, south of Route 9; West River floodplain a quarter mile away; powerline cut forming a wildlife corridor along the back fence.
Time: Late summer into early fall, present day
Theme: Human–wildlife coexistence; consequences of indulgence; boundaries and care; rural economics vs. compassion; ecology of small farms
Tone: Grounded, observational, quietly tense with moments of tenderness
Point of View: Close third alternating between Maeve and Eli; occasional tightly limited sensory passages from the rabbit without anthropomorphic thoughts - Maeve chapters track secrecy, nursing routines, and outreach to Lila; Eli chapters track hive management, cash flow, and fence/bear problems; rabbit passages center on vibration, scent, pressure, and heat under the hives. 
Characters:  - Maeve Kelleher — 34, co-owner, marketing/processing, secretly feeding an injured cottontail, craving to nurture; former CNA who left after pandemic burnout; drives a gray Subaru with a cracked taillight; keeps a stash of romaine, parsley, and black oil sunflower seed in the warm room; old scar on her wrist from a swarm cutout; reads rehab blogs at night; rationalizes “only until it heals.” - Eli Barrows — 37, third‑generation beekeeper, pragmatic, cash‑strapped, prioritizes hive survival and fences; runs mite washes by the book; carries a spiral notebook of expenses; owes on a line of credit and a new extractor; uses bacon-greased foil on the fence to “educate” bears; right shoulder tendonitis from lifting supers; wary of social media; sleeps lightly with a flashlight by the door. - The Rabbit — adult eastern cottontail with a torn hind leg; fur clumped at the flank from botfly warble; favors the cool dust under pallet runners, memorizes the thrum pattern of the strongest hives; scars from a wire snag along the hindpastern; uses zig‑zag bursts when flushed; heat-seeks to the south‑facing block wall at dawn. - Ruth Barrows — Eli’s widowed mother, old‑school frugality, urges hard choices; keeps receipts in a cookie tin; measures compassion in jars sold; tells stories of her late husband shooting a marauding dog as cautionary lore; brings casseroles and tough love; quietly pays an overdue diesel bill once. - Lila Martinez — local wildlife rehabilitator, firm on non‑feeding boundaries; runs a small permitted facility; keeps a binder of state regs; arrives with a headlamp and hemostats for botfly removal only under proper conditions; insists on brush cover creation, no food; warns of tularemia and legal liability; drives a dented Tacoma with crates. - Gary Dutton — state game warden, cites and warns about wildlife feeding; mid‑40s, patient tone; leaves a written warning after Aiden’s post; explains escalation to fines and confiscation; checks electric fence compliance; notes bear sign and suggests removing attractants including spilled cappings and chicken feed from neighbors. - Henry Pike — neighboring vegetable grower, sets box traps after crop losses; pragmatic to curt; shows chewed kale stems; sets Havahart traps along the hedgerow; pressures Eli to “handle it” or he’ll call the paper; a past grievance over borrowed pallets colors interactions. - Aiden Cho — 16, part‑time farmhand, social‑media posts escalate attention and foot traffic; clever with reels; films a dusk clip of the rabbit under the hives that goes semi‑viral locally; brings uninvited followers and rabbit snacks; later flies a drone that spooks Soot and flushes the rabbit; gets benched after the warden visit. - Soot — aging border collie mix, unpredictably flushes wildlife; mostly deaf, arthritic; reliable with sheep at Ruth’s but unreliable on “leave it”; noses spilled cappings; triggers one panicked flight of the rabbit into the fence line. - Mara Levin — food co‑op buyer, presses for steady supply and fall promo commitments; wants “bear‑safe apiary” talking points and a tasting table in October; threatens to shift shelf space if deliveries falter; offers a holiday feature if they can guarantee stock. 
Premise: As Maeve quietly nurses a limping wild rabbit that has begun sheltering beneath the hives on the small honey farm she runs with Eli outside Brattleboro, her compassion collides with Eli’s need to protect their bees and fall honey pull; the feeding she starts brings predators and a bear to the fence line, damages gear, and risks their year’s income, forcing the pair to decide what care looks like—habitat and hard boundaries, relocation, or a kill—as first frost nears and the farm’s ecology and finances tighten.
